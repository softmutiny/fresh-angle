# Calibration examples

Use these examples to distinguish actual information gain from polished repetition. The examples are fictional.

## Weak: a generic label

**Context:** A person delays publishing essays, reorganizes notes repeatedly, and discards drafts after rereading them.

**Weak answer:** “You are a perfectionist who fears failure. Try to be kinder to yourself.”

Why it fails:

- The label is generic.
- It does not distinguish fear of failure from fear of misrepresentation, loss of control, or simple lack of interest.
- It makes no testable prediction.
- The advice is not tied to a mechanism.

## Weak: an elegant echo

**User framing:** “I think I keep planning because planning feels safer than acting.”

**Weak answer:** “Your plans are not preparation; they are a shelter from uncertainty.”

Why it fails: the sentence is attractive, but it only restates the user's conclusion.

## Weak: a universal truth presented as personal insight

**Context:** A person prefers receiving several options and often sets an ambitious target before accepting a more moderate result.

**Weak answer:** “You value choice, and you aim high because higher goals improve your eventual result.”

Why it fails:

- Most people prefer having useful choices and understand that ambitious targets can raise outcomes.
- The answer would sound equally plausible if the user were replaced with a random stranger.
- It does not identify when this person wants options, when options become burdensome, or what differentiates their use of ambitious targets.
- Its prediction is merely the original observation repeated into the future.

## Strong: mechanism, prediction, lever

**Context:** A person happily writes private notes, freezes when choosing a public title, and resumes writing after deciding a post can be revised later.

**Candidate insight:** The bottleneck may be perceived identity commitment rather than effort or criticism. Publishing feels like permanently declaring “this is what I believe,” while private writing stays reversible.

**Prediction:** The person will find a small post with a permanent profile placement harder than a longer message in a temporary or editable space, even when the expected audience is similar.

**Lever:** Separate publication from identity commitment: label posts as versions, keep an explicit revision policy, and test whether output rises without reducing editorial standards.

**Falsifier:** If equally editable and reversible publication still produces the same freezing, identity commitment is probably not the main mechanism.

Why it passes: it connects three observations, distinguishes competing explanations, predicts an unseen preference, proposes a mechanism-specific experiment, and identifies the user's distinctive sensitivity to perceived permanence rather than a generic dislike of difficult work.

## Correct refusal

**Context:** “I changed jobs once and felt relieved. Tell me my deepest recurring pattern.”

**Good response:** “One event is not enough to establish a recurring personal mechanism. I can analyze this decision locally, or look for a stable pattern after comparing it with other choices.”

Why it passes: refusing to manufacture depth protects the purpose of the skill.
